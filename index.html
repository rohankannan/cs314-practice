Problem 1

Short answers

15 pts≈ 8 minLike pop quizzes 1–2 and the Lecture 1 and 7 demos

Answer each part in one to three sentences. Each part is worth 3 points.

  1. The toy floating-point system from Lectures 3–4 uses 5 bits: 1 sign bit, 2 exponent bits and 2 fraction bits. Exponent field 00 holds zero and the subnormals \((0.b_1b_2)_2\times 2^{0}\), field 11 holds Inf and NaN, and the bias is 1, so the normalized numbers are \(\pm(1.b_1b_2)_2\times 2^{e}\) with \(e\in\{0,1\}\). Using round to nearest (ties to even), find \(\mathrm{fl}(2.25)\), \(\mathrm{fl}(3.3)\) and \(\mathrm{fl}(0.6)\).
  2. In MATLAB, which uses IEEE double precision: 
    >> 10^20 + 10 - 10^20
ans =
     0
>> 10^20 - 10^20 + 10
ans =
    10
    Explain the first result using the spacing of doubles near \(10^{20}\).
  3. Pop quiz 2 asked this. The normalized binary representation of a float, \(1.b_1\ldots b_t\times 2^e\), is unique. Is the normalized binary representation of a real number also unique? Give a reason or an example.
  4. Bisection is called a global method, while Newton's method and the secant method are called local methods. What do these words mean here, and how does MATLAB's fzero use this?
  5. In Lecture 7, Newton's method applied to \(f(x)=(x-3)^4\) from \(x_0=4\) printed: 
     1   3.7500000000000000
 2   3.5625000000000000
 3   3.4218750000000000
 4   3.3164062500000000
 ...
20   3.0031712119389340
    What is the rate of convergence? Why isn't it quadratic?
Show solutionHide solution

(a) The positive floats are the subnormals 0.25, 0.5, 0.75, then 1, 1.25, 1.5, 1.75 (\(e=0\), spacing 0.25) and 2, 2.5, 3, 3.5 (\(e=1\), spacing 0.5).

  • \(2.25=(1.00\,|\,1)_2\times2^1\) is exactly halfway between \(2=(1.00)_2\times2^1\) and \(2.5=(1.01)_2\times2^1\). A tie goes to the neighbor whose last bit is 0, so \(\mathrm{fl}(2.25)=2\).
  • 3.3 is 0.2 from 3.5 and 0.3 from 3, so \(\mathrm{fl}(3.3)=3.5\).
  • 0.6 is 0.1 from 0.5 and 0.15 from 0.75, so \(\mathrm{fl}(0.6)=0.5\).

Why it mattersHere \(u=2^{-(t+1)}=2^{-3}=0.125\), yet \(\mathrm{fl}(0.6)\) has relative error \(0.1/0.6\approx0.167>u\). The bound \(|\delta|\le u\) only holds in the normalized range. Subnormals have fewer significant bits.

(b) MATLAB evaluates left to right. Since \(2^{66}\approx7.4\times10^{19}\le10^{20}\lt2^{67}\), doubles near \(10^{20}\) have the form \((1.b_1\ldots b_{52})_2\times2^{66}\) and are spaced \(2^{66-52}=2^{14}=16384\) apart. So \(10^{20}+10\) is within half a gap (8192) of \(10^{20}\) and rounds back to \(10^{20}\); subtracting \(10^{20}\) then gives 0. In the second line \(10^{20}-10^{20}=0\) exactly, and \(0+10=10\).

Why it mattersAdding a small number to a much larger one can leave the large one unchanged, so floating-point addition is not associative.

(c) No. For example \(1=(1.000\ldots)_2\times2^0=(1.111\ldots)_2\times2^{-1}\), because \((0.111\ldots)_2=\tfrac12+\tfrac14+\tfrac18+\cdots=1\). Any real number with a terminating binary expansion also has an expansion ending in infinitely many 1s. A float stores only \(t\) fraction bits, so it can't have an infinite tail, and its representation is unique.

(d) Global: bisection converges whenever \(f\) is continuous on \([a,b]\) and \(\operatorname{sign}f(a)\ne\operatorname{sign}f(b)\), wherever the root is, because each step keeps an interval with a sign change (Intermediate Value Theorem). Its drawback is speed: the error bound only halves each step. Local: Newton and secant converge fast (quadratic, and order \(\approx1.618\)) but only when the starting guess or guesses are close enough to the root. From a poor start they can diverge or cycle.

fzero is a safeguarded method. It combines bisection with the secant method and inverse quadratic interpolation: it takes the fast steps when they stay inside a bracketing interval and falls back on bisection otherwise.

(e) The errors are 1, 0.75, 0.5625, 0.421875, …, and each is \(\tfrac34\) of the one before: linear convergence with \(C=\tfrac34\). Algebraically,

\[x_{k+1}-3=(x_k-3)-\frac{(x_k-3)^4}{4(x_k-3)^3}=\tfrac34\,(x_k-3).\]

The quadratic convergence theorem assumes \(f'(x_*)\ne0\). Here \(x_*=3\) is a root of multiplicity 4, so \(f'(3)=0\). For a root of multiplicity \(m\), Newton's error shrinks by the factor \(1-\tfrac1m\) each step, which is why 20 iterations only reached 3.0032.

Problem 2

Binary and integer representations

12 pts≈ 7 minLike HW1 #2, #3, #6 and HW2 #1(a)
  1. [4 pts] Write 0.3 as a normalized binary number correctly rounded to 6 bits after the binary point. What is the decimal value of the stored number? Is it larger or smaller than 0.3?
  2. [3 pts] Convert \(x=(0.\overline{011})_2=(0.011011011\ldots)_2\) to a fraction.
  3. [3 pts] MATLAB's int8 type stores integers in 8-bit two's complement. Give the decimal value and bit pattern of intmax('int8') and intmin('int8'), and the bit pattern of \(-37\).
  4. [2 pts] An IEEE double has these fields: sign, exponent (11 bits), fraction (52 bits, remaining bits all 0).
    1100000000101010 1000 0000 … 0000
    What number is it?
Show solutionHide solution

(a) Multiply the fraction by 2 and record the integer parts: \(0.3\to0.6\) (0), \(1.2\) (1), \(0.4\) (0), \(0.8\) (0), \(1.6\) (1), \(1.2\) (1), and then 0.4 comes back, so the block 1001 repeats:

\[0.3=(0.0\overline{1001})_2=(0.0100110011\ldots)_2=(1.001100\,|\,110011\ldots)_2\times2^{-2}.\]

The first discarded bit is 1 and the bits after it are not all 0, so the tail is more than half a unit in the last place. Round up:

\[\mathrm{fl}(0.3)=(1.001101)_2\times2^{-2}=\frac{1+\frac18+\frac1{16}+\frac1{64}}{4}=\frac{1.203125}{4}=0.30078125.\]

\((1.001101)_2\times2^{-2}=0.30078125\), which is larger than 0.3 (relative error 0.26%, below \(u=2^{-7}\approx0.78\%\)).

(b) Multiply by \(2^3=8\) to shift one full period left of the point: \(8x=(11.\overline{011})_2=3+x\). The repeating tails cancel, so \(7x=3\) and \(x=\tfrac37\). Check: doubling \(\tfrac37\) gives bits 0, 1, 1 and then \(\tfrac37\) again.

(c) The leading bit has weight \(-2^7=-128\) and the others are positive.

  • intmax \(=\) 01111111 \(=2^7-1=\) 127
  • intmin \(=\) 10000000 \(=\) −128
  • \(-37\): write \(37=\) 00100101, flip every bit to get 11011010, add 1: 11011011. Check: \(-128+64+16+8+2+1=-37\).

(d) Exponent field \(=1024+2=1026\), so \(e=1026-1023=3\). With the hidden bit, the significand is \((1.10101)_2=1+\tfrac12+\tfrac18+\tfrac1{32}=1.65625\). The sign bit is 1, so the value is \(-1.65625\times2^3=\) \(-13.25\).

Problem 3

A 10-bit floating-point system

20 pts≈ 12 minLike HW1 #4 and Lectures 4–5

A float in this system has 10 bits:

ssign, 1 bit
eeeeexponent, 4 bits
ffffffraction, t = 5 bits

It follows the IEEE design from class: a hidden bit for normalized numbers \(\pm(1.f)_2\times2^e\); exponent field 0000 for zero and the subnormals \(\pm(0.f)_2\times2^{e_{\min}}\); field 1111 for Inf and NaN; and a bias, so that \(e=\) (stored field) \(-\) bias. Rounding is round to nearest, ties to even.

  1. [2 pts] What is the bias, and what range of exponents \(e\) do normalized numbers use?
  2. [4 pts] Find realmax, realmin and the smallest positive subnormal number, as exact decimals.
  3. [2 pts] How many positive normalized numbers are there? How many positive subnormals?
  4. [3 pts] What is the unit roundoff \(u\)? What is the distance from 1 to the next larger float? What is the largest gap between two consecutive floats?
  5. [3 pts] Find \(\mathrm{fl}(20.75)\) and its relative error. Check the error against \(u\).
  6. [6 pts] Let \(x=1\) and \(y=(1.11111)_2\times2^{-1}\). The exact difference is \(x-y=2^{-6}\).
    1. Compute \(x-y\) on a machine that keeps only 5 bits after the binary point while it shifts \(y\) to line up with \(x\)'s exponent, so bits shifted past the 5th place are lost. Normalize the result. What is its relative error?
    2. Repeat with one extra (guard) bit.
    3. What does this tell you about the property \(\mathrm{fl}(x-y)=(x-y)(1+\delta)\), \(|\delta|\le u\)?
Show solutionHide solution

(a) The 4-bit field stores 0 to 15. The extremes 0 and 15 are reserved, so fields 1 to 14 are used, and the bias is \(14/2=7\) (the same as \(2^{4-1}-1\)). So bias \(=7\) and \(e\in[1-7,\,14-7]=[-6,\,7]\).

(b)

  • realmax \(=(1.11111)_2\times2^{7}=(2-2^{-5})\times128=\) 252
  • realmin \(=(1.00000)_2\times2^{-6}=\) \(2^{-6}=0.015625\)
  • smallest subnormal \(=(0.00001)_2\times2^{-6}=2^{-5}\cdot2^{-6}=\) \(2^{-11}=0.00048828125\) (about \(4.9\times10^{-4}\))

(c) Normalized: 14 exponent values times \(2^5=32\) fractions gives 448. Subnormals: field 0000 with any of the \(2^5-1=\) 31 nonzero fractions.

Check: with both signs and \(\pm0\), \(2(448+31)+2=960=2^{10}-2\cdot2^{5}\), which is every bit pattern except the 64 with field 1111.

(d) \(u=2^{-(t+1)}=2^{-6}=0.015625\). The next float after 1 is \((1.00001)_2=1+2^{-5}\), so the gap is \(2^{-5}=0.03125\) \((=2u)\). The largest gap is in the top binade \([2^7,2^8)\): \(2^{-5}\cdot2^{7}=4\), for example between 248 and 252.

(e) \(20.75=(10100.11)_2=(1.010011)_2\times2^4\). Keeping 5 bits leaves \((1.01001\,|\,1)_2\): the discarded part is exactly half a unit, a tie. The neighbors are \((1.01001)_2\times2^4=20.5\) and \((1.01010)_2\times2^4=21\). Ties to even picks the one whose last bit is 0: \(\mathrm{fl}(20.75)=21\).

Relative error \(=0.25/20.75\approx0.0120\le u=0.015625\). ✓

(f)

(i) Shifting \(y\) to exponent 0 gives \((0.111111)_2\), which needs 6 bits. Keeping 5 leaves \((0.11111)_2\); the last 1 falls off the end.

\[\begin{array}{r@{\;}l} & 1.00000\\ - & 0.11111\\ \hline & 0.00001\end{array}\qquad\Rightarrow\quad (1.00000)_2\times2^{-5}=0.03125.\]

The exact answer is \(2^{-6}\), so the relative error is \((2^{-5}-2^{-6})/2^{-6}=\) 1, i.e. 100%.

(ii) With a guard bit: \(1.000000-0.111111=0.000001=2^{-6}\), which normalizes to \((1.00000)_2\times2^{-6}\). Exact.

(iii) Without extra bits, a subtraction can have relative error far larger than \(u\), so \(\mathrm{fl}(x-y)=(x-y)(1+\delta)\) with \(|\delta|\le u\) fails. With extra bits (IEEE uses 2 guard bits and 1 sticky bit), every operation returns the exact result rounded to \(t\) bits, so the property holds.

(b) Multiply by the conjugate (equivalently, use \(x_1x_2=c/a\)):

\[\begin{aligned} x_1&=\frac{-b+\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{2a}\cdot\frac{-b-\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}{-b-\sqrt{b^2-4ac}}\\ &=\frac{b^2-(b^2-4ac)}{2a\left(-b-\sqrt{b^2-4ac}\right)}=\frac{-2c}{\,b+\sqrt{b^2-4ac}\,}.\end{aligned}\]

Same answer the Lecture 6 way: \(x_2=(-89.30-89.27)/2=-178.6/2=-89.30\), then \(x_1=c/(a\,x_2)=1/(-89.30)=-0.01120\).

(c) The subtraction \(-b+\sqrt{b^2-4ac}\). Because \(b^2\gg4ac\), \(\sqrt{b^2-4ac}\approx b\) and the leading digits cancel. The subtraction itself is exact, but the small rounding errors already made in \(b^2\) and in the square root (each under 0.01%) become most of what is left, a 34% relative error. For \(x_2\) (with \(b\gt0\)), \(-b-\sqrt{b^2-4ac}\) adds two numbers of the same sign, so nothing cancels.

Rule from classIf \(b\gt0\), compute \(x_2=\frac{-b-\sqrt{D}}{2a}\) first and then \(x_1=\frac{c}{a\,x_2}\). If \(b\lt0\), compute \(x_1=\frac{-b+\sqrt D}{2a}\) first and then \(x_2=\frac{c}{a\,x_1}\).

(d) \(b^2=3.6\times10^{401}\) and \(4ac=2.4\times10^{401}\) both overflow to Inf, and \(\text{Inf}-\text{Inf}=\text{NaN}\). So both roots come out NaN, even though the roots themselves are ordinary numbers.

Then \(2x^2+6x+3=0\) gives \(x=\frac{-3\pm\sqrt3}{2}\), so \(x_1\approx-0.6340\), \(x_2\approx-2.3660\) (using the cancellation-free formulas).

Problem 6

Newton's method

12 pts≈ 8 minLike HW2 #3 and #5, Lectures 7–8
  1. [3 pts] Derive Newton's method from the Taylor series of \(f\) about \(x_k\) with the remainder term, as in Lecture 7.
  2. [5 pts] Write Newton's iteration for \(A^{1/3}\), the root of \(f(x)=x^3-A\). With \(A=2\) and \(x_0=1\), compute \(x_1\), \(x_2\), \(x_3\). Bisection on \([1,2]\) needs 24 steps to get \(2^{1/3}\) to 7 decimal places. Roughly how many Newton steps does it take? (\(2^{1/3}=1.259921049\ldots\))
  3. [4 pts] Write Newton's method as a fixed-point iteration \(x_{k+1}=g(x_k)\). Show that \(g'(x_*)=0\) at a root with \(f'(x_*)\ne0\), and use a Taylor expansion of \(g\) about \(x_*\) to show that \(e_{k+1}\le C\,e_k^2\).
Show solutionHide solution

(a) Taylor's theorem about the reference point \(x_k\), evaluated at the root \(x_*\):

\[0=f(x_*)=f(x_k)+(x_*-x_k)\,f'(x_k)+\frac{(x_*-x_k)^2}{2}\,f''(s),\qquad s\text{ between }x_k\text{ and }x_*.\]

We can't solve this for \(x_*\) because \(s\) is unknown. Drop the remainder and call the solution \(x_{k+1}\):

\[0=f(x_k)+(x_{k+1}-x_k)\,f'(x_k)\;\Longrightarrow\;x_{k+1}=x_k-\frac{f(x_k)}{f'(x_k)},\quad f'(x_k)\ne0.\]

Geometrically, \(x_{k+1}\) is where the tangent line at \((x_k,f(x_k))\) crosses the \(x\)-axis.

(b) \(f'(x)=3x^2\), so \(x_{k+1}=x_k-\dfrac{x_k^3-A}{3x_k^2}=\dfrac{2x_k^3+A}{3x_k^2}\).

k\(x_k\)\(e_k\)\(e_k/e_{k-1}^2\)

The number of correct digits roughly doubles each step: \(x_3\) has 4 correct decimals and \(x_4\) has 9. So about 4 Newton steps do the work of 24 bisection steps.

The ratio \(e_k/e_{k-1}^2\) settles at \(\left|\dfrac{f''(x_*)}{2f'(x_*)}\right|=\dfrac{6x_*}{6x_*^2}=\dfrac1{x_*}\approx0.794\), the signature of quadratic convergence.

(c) \(g(x)=x-\dfrac{f(x)}{f'(x)}\). By the quotient rule,

\[g'(x)=1-\frac{f'(x)^2-f(x)\,f''(x)}{f'(x)^2}=\frac{f(x)\,f''(x)}{f'(x)^2},\qquad g'(x_*)=\frac{0\cdot f''(x_*)}{f'(x_*)^2}=0.\]

Expand \(g(x_k)\) about \(x_*\), using \(g(x_k)=x_{k+1}\), \(g(x_*)=x_*\) and \(g'(x_*)=0\):

\[x_{k+1}-x_*=g(x_k)-g(x_*)=\underbrace{g'(x_*)}_{0}(x_k-x_*)+\frac{g''(s)}{2}(x_k-x_*)^2.\]

Taking absolute values, \(e_{k+1}=\dfrac{|g''(s)|}{2}\,e_k^2\le C\,e_k^2\) with \(C=\tfrac12\max|g''|\) on an interval around \(x_*\). This holds once \(x_0\) is in an interval around \(x_*\) where \(|g'|\lt1\), so the iterates stay close to the root.

  3. [2 pts] The secant method has lower order than Newton's method. Give two reasons someone might still prefer it.
Show solutionHide solution

(a) \(f(1)=-2\), \(f(2)=1\).

\[x_2=x_1-\frac{f(x_1)(x_1-x_0)}{f(x_1)-f(x_0)}=2-\frac{(1)(1)}{1-(-2)}=\frac53\approx1.6666667\]

\(f(x_2)=\frac{25}9-3=-\frac29\approx-0.2222222\), so

\[x_3=\frac53-\frac{\left(-\frac29\right)\left(-\frac13\right)}{-\frac29-1}=\frac53+\frac2{33}=\frac{19}{11}\approx1.7272727.\]

\(x_2=\tfrac53\approx1.6666667\), \(x_3=\tfrac{19}{11}\approx1.7272727\), so \(|x_3-\sqrt3|\approx0.0048\). The errors are \(0.268,\ 0.065,\ 0.0048\), and the next step gives \(x_4=\tfrac{97}{56}\approx1.7321429\) with error \(9.2\times10^{-5}\).

This matches \(e_{k+1}\approx C\,e_k\,e_{k-1}\) with \(C=\left|\frac{f''}{2f'}\right|=\frac1{2\sqrt3}\approx0.289\): \(0.289\times0.065\times0.268\approx0.0051\). For \(f=x^2-a\), the secant step simplifies to \(x_{k+1}=\frac{x_kx_{k-1}+a}{x_k+x_{k-1}}\).

(b) Estimate the order with \(r\approx\dfrac{\log(e_{k+1}/e_k)}{\log(e_k/e_{k-1})}\).

  • P: \(e_{k+1}/e_k=0.7\) every step, so linear, \(C=0.7\). For example, fixed-point iteration with \(|g'(x_*)|=0.7\).
  • Q: the exponents 1, 2, 4, 8, 16 double, so \(e_{k+1}=e_k^2\): quadratic, order 2. Newton at a simple root.
  • R: the exponents 1, 2, 3, 5, 8, 13 are Fibonacci numbers, so \(e_{k+1}=e_k\,e_{k-1}\). The exponent ratios 1.5, 1.67, 1.6, 1.625 approach \(\frac{1+\sqrt5}2\approx1.618\), superlinear. The secant method.

(c) Any two of these:

  • It needs no derivative, which may be unavailable or expensive.
  • It needs only one new function value per step, while Newton needs \(f\) and \(f'\). If \(f'\) costs as much as \(f\), two secant steps cost the same as one Newton step and raise the error to the power \(1.618^2\approx2.618\gt2\), so secant is often faster per unit of work.

Both methods are local. Secant needs two starting guesses.

An iteration that runs away

Like HW2 #1, with Lecture 8's fixed-point theory

In exact arithmetic, \(x=\tfrac13\) is a fixed point of \(x\leftarrow4x-1\), since \(\tfrac43-1=\tfrac13\). Store \(x_0=\tfrac13\) with \(t=4\) bits after the binary point (normalized, round to nearest), then apply \(x\leftarrow4x-1\) repeatedly in the same 4-bit arithmetic. What happens, and why?

Show solutionHide solution

\(\tfrac13=(0.010101\ldots)_2=(1.0101\,|\,0101\ldots)_2\times2^{-2}\). The first discarded bit is 0, so round down: \(x_0=(1.0101)_2\times2^{-2}=0.328125\).

Multiplying by 4 only changes the exponent: \(4x_0=(1.0101)_2=1.3125\). Subtracting 1 leaves \((0.0101)_2=(1.0100)_2\times2^{-2}=0.3125\); normalizing shifts left and pads a 0 that carries no information about \(\tfrac13\).

k\(x_k\)error \(x_k-\tfrac13\)
00.328125−0.0052
10.3125−0.0208
20.25−0.0833

4−1−1.3333
5−5−5.3333

The error is multiplied by exactly 4 each step, and the iterates run off to \(-\infty\). As a fixed-point iteration, \(g(x)=4x-1\) has \(g'(x)=4\), so \(|g'(x_*)|\gt1\) and the fixed point repels. The arithmetic in these steps is exact; all the damage comes from the tiny initial rounding of \(\tfrac13\).

Extra 2

Why the sticky bit matters

Like Lecture 5

Use \(t=3\) fraction bits and round to nearest, ties to even. Compute \(1+(1.001)_2\times2^{-4}\):

  1. exactly, then rounded to 3 bits;
  2. with 2 guard bits but no sticky bit;
  3. with 2 guard bits and a sticky bit.
Show solutionHide solution

\(y=(1.001)_2\times2^{-4}=(0.0001001)_2=0.0703125\).

(i) Exact sum \((1.0001001)_2=1.0703125\). Rounded to 3 bits: \((1.000\,|\,1001)_2\). The tail is more than half, so round up to \((1.001)_2=1.125\).

(ii) Aligning \(y\) keeps \(3+2=5\) bits, \((0.00010)_2\); the final 1 is lost. The sum \((1.00010)_2\) looks like an exact tie between 1.000 and 1.001, and ties to even gives 1.000, the wrong answer.

(iii) The sticky bit records that a nonzero bit was shifted off, so \((1.00010)_2\) with sticky 1 means "slightly more than half". Round up: 1.125, correct.

IEEE requires every result to equal the exact result correctly rounded. Guard bits alone can't tell an exact tie from "just above a tie"; the sticky bit can.

Extra 3

Newton without division

Like HW2 #5

Derive Newton's iteration for \(1/\sqrt R\) from \(f(x)=\dfrac1{x^2}-R\), and simplify so the only division is by 2 (an exponent shift). With \(R=2\) and \(x_0=0.7\), compute \(x_1\) and \(x_2\). Then show that \(r_k=1-Rx_k^2\) satisfies \(r_{k+1}=\tfrac14r_k^2(3+r_k)\), and explain what that says about the convergence.

Show solutionHide solution

\(f'(x)=-2/x^3\), so

\[x_{k+1}=x_k-\frac{x_k^{-2}-R}{-2x_k^{-3}}=x_k+\frac{x_k-Rx_k^3}{2}=\frac{x_k\,(3-Rx_k^2)}{2}.\]

With \(R=2\): \(x_1=0.7(3-0.98)/2=0.707\) and \(x_2=0.707(3-0.999698)/2=0.70710676\). The true value is \(1/\sqrt2=0.70710678\).

Let \(s=Rx_k^2=1-r_k\). Then \(Rx_{k+1}^2=\tfrac14s(3-s)^2=\tfrac14(1-r_k)(2+r_k)^2=1-\tfrac34r_k^2-\tfrac14r_k^3\), so

\[r_{k+1}=\frac{r_k^2\,(3+r_k)}{4}\approx\tfrac34\,r_k^2.\]

The relative error is squared (times about \(\tfrac34\)) each step: quadratic convergence. Here \(r_0=0.02\), \(r_1=3.0\times10^{-4}\), \(r_2=6.8\times10^{-8}\).

To keep \(x_1\gt0\) you need \(Rx_0^2\lt3\), that is \(0\lt x_0\lt\sqrt{3/R}\).

How good is the fixed-point bound?

Like Lecture 8

For \(g_A(x)=\dfrac{x^2+5}{6}\) on \([0,2]\): show that \(g_A\) maps \([0,2]\) into itself and that \(|g_A'(x)|\le\tfrac23\) there. Use \(e_k\le\rho^k e_0\) to find how many iterations guarantee \(|x_k-1|\le10^{-6}\) from any \(x_0\in[0,2]\). Compare with how many it actually takes.

Show solutionHide solution

\(g_A'(x)=x/3\ge0\), so \(g_A\) is increasing and \(g_A([0,2])=[g_A(0),g_A(2)]=[\tfrac56,\tfrac32]\subset[0,2]\). On \([0,2]\), \(|g_A'(x)|=x/3\le\tfrac23=\rho\).

From Lecture 8, \(e_{k+1}=|g'(s_k)|\,e_k\le\rho\,e_k\), so \(e_k\le\rho^k e_0\le(\tfrac23)^k\cdot1\), since every \(x_0\in[0,2]\) is within 1 of the fixed point 1.

\[\left(\tfrac23\right)^k\le10^{-6}\iff k\ge\frac{6}{\log_{10}1.5}\approx34.07,\]

so 35 iterations are guaranteed to suffice. In practice it takes 12 from \(x_0=0\) and 14 from \(x_0=2\), because near the root the error shrinks by \(|g_A'(1)|=\tfrac13\) per step, not \(\tfrac23\). The bound uses the worst slope on the whole interval.

Extra 5

Where 1.618 comes from

Like Lecture 9

Suppose the secant errors satisfy \(e_{k+1}\approx C\,e_k\,e_{k-1}\), and that \(e_{k+1}\approx A\,e_k^{\alpha}\) for constants \(A\) and \(\alpha\). Find \(\alpha\).

Show solutionHide solution

From \(e_k\approx A\,e_{k-1}^{\alpha}\), \(e_{k-1}\approx(e_k/A)^{1/\alpha}\). Substitute into \(A\,e_k^\alpha\approx C\,e_k\,e_{k-1}\):

\[A\,e_k^{\alpha}\approx C\,e_k\left(\frac{e_k}{A}\right)^{1/\alpha}\;\Longrightarrow\;\frac{A^{1+1/\alpha}}{C}\approx e_k^{\,1+1/\alpha-\alpha}.\]

The left side doesn't depend on \(k\), but \(e_k\to0\), so the exponent must be 0:

\[1+\frac1\alpha-\alpha=0\iff\alpha^2-\alpha-1=0\iff\alpha=\frac{1\pm\sqrt5}{2}.\]

Take the positive root so the error decreases: \(\alpha=\frac{1+\sqrt5}2\approx1.618\).

Extra 6

When Newton fails

Like Lecture 7
  1. Apply Newton's method to \(f(x)=x^3-2x+2\) from \(x_0=0\).
  2. Apply Newton's method to \(f(x)=\arctan x\) from \(x_0=1\), and again from \(x_0=2\). Explain the difference.
Show solutionHide solution

(i) \(f'(x)=3x^2-2\). \(x_1=0-\frac{2}{-2}=1\), then \(x_2=1-\frac{1-2+2}{3-2}=0\). The iterates cycle 0, 1, 0, 1, … forever. From \(x_0=-2\) it converges to the real root: \(-1.8,\ -1.76995,\ -1.76929,\ldots\to-1.7692924\).

(ii) \(x_{k+1}=x_k-(1+x_k^2)\arctan x_k\) (radians).

  • From \(x_0=1\): \(-0.5708,\ 0.1169,\ -0.0011,\ 0.0000\). Converges to 0, fast.
  • From \(x_0=2\): \(-3.5357,\ 13.951,\ -279.34,\ldots\) Diverges. The graph of \(\arctan\) is so flat out there that each tangent line overshoots farther.

The dividing line is \(x_0\approx1.3917\), where the iterates bounce between \(\pm x_0\). Newton is a local method; it also breaks down when \(f'(x_k)\approx0\), because the step becomes huge or undefined. Safeguarded methods like fzero keep a bracket to avoid this.

IEEE double facts

Like the Lecture 1, 2 and 4 MATLAB demos

Answer without MATLAB.

  1. What are realmax, realmin and the smallest subnormal?
  2. Why is realmax + 1 == realmax true, while realmax + 2^970 is Inf?
  3. What is 2^-1075?
  4. Lecture 1 compared 1 + (3e-16 + 3e-16) with (1 + 3e-16) + 3e-16. Which is larger, and what are the two values?
Show solutionHide solution

(i) realmax \(=(2-2^{-52})\times2^{1023}\approx1.8\times10^{308}\); realmin \(=2^{-1022}\approx2.2\times10^{-308}\); smallest subnormal \(=2^{-52}\times2^{-1022}=2^{-1074}\approx4.9\times10^{-324}\).

(ii) The gap just below realmax is \(2^{-52}\times2^{1023}=2^{971}\). Adding 1, far less than half the gap, rounds back to realmax. But realmax \(+\,2^{970}\) is exactly halfway between realmax and \(2^{1024}\). Realmax's last fraction bit is 1 (odd), so ties to even rounds up to \(2^{1024}\), which overflows to Inf.

(iii) \(2^{-1075}\) is exactly half the smallest subnormal, a tie between 0 and \(2^{-1074}\). Ties to even gives 0 (underflow).

(iv) Near 1, doubles are spaced \(2^{-52}\approx2.22\times10^{-16}\). \(1+3\times10^{-16}\) rounds to \(1+2^{-52}\); adding \(3\times10^{-16}\) again lands at \(1+5.2\times10^{-16}\), which rounds to \(1+2\cdot2^{-52}=1.0000000000000004\). The other order adds \(3\times10^{-16}+3\times10^{-16}=6\times10^{-16}\) first, and \(1+6\times10^{-16}\) rounds to \(1+3\cdot2^{-52}=\) 1.0000000000000007, the larger one (and closer to the exact sum).

Deriving the unit roundoff bound

Like Lecture 5

Let \(x=(1.b_1b_2\ldots b_tb_{t+1}\ldots)_2\times2^e\), and let \(\mathrm{fl}(x)\) be \(x\) rounded to nearest with \(t\) bits after the binary point. Show that \(\dfrac{|\mathrm{fl}(x)-x|}{|x|}\le2^{-(t+1)}\).

Show solutionHide solution

\(x\) lies between two consecutive floats, \(x_1=(1.b_1\ldots b_t)_2\times2^e\) and \(x_2=x_1+2^{-t}\times2^e\). Round to nearest picks the closer one, so the error is at most half the gap:

\[|\mathrm{fl}(x)-x|\le\tfrac12\cdot2^{-t}\cdot2^{e}=2^{-(t+1)}\,2^{e}.\]

Divide by \(|x|=(1.b_1b_2\ldots)_2\times2^e\ge2^e\):

\[\frac{|\mathrm{fl}(x)-x|}{|x|}\le\frac{2^{-(t+1)}\,2^{e}}{2^{e}}=2^{-(t+1)}=u.\]

Equivalently \(\mathrm{fl}(x)=x(1+\delta)\) with \(|\delta|\le u\). For double precision, \(t=52\) gives \(u=2^{-53}\approx1.1\times10^{-16}\). The argument needs \(x\) in the normalized range; subnormals break it, as Problem 1(a) showed.

Taylor series with remainder

Like pop quiz 3 and Lecture 7

Write the degree-2 Taylor polynomial of \(f(x)=\sqrt x\) about \(x_0=4\), with the remainder term. Use it to approximate \(\sqrt{4.1}\) and bound the error.

Show solutionHide solution

\(f(4)=2\); \(f'(x)=\frac1{2\sqrt x}\to\frac14\); \(f''(x)=-\frac1{4x^{3/2}}\to-\frac1{32}\); \(f'''(x)=\frac3{8x^{5/2}}\). With \(h=x-4\):

\[\sqrt{4+h}=2+\frac h4-\frac{h^2}{64}+\frac{h^3}{6}\cdot\frac{3}{8\,s^{5/2}},\qquad s\in[4,\,4+h].\]

With \(h=0.1\): \(2+0.025-0.00015625=\) 2.02484375. Since \(f'''\) is largest at \(s=4\), the error is at most \(\frac{0.1^3}{6}\cdot\frac{3}{8\cdot32}\approx\) \(1.95\times10^{-6}\). (Actual: \(\sqrt{4.1}=2.0248457\), error \(1.92\times10^{-6}\).)

Note-sheet checklist

If you can derive these, you only need a line for each. If you can't, that's the part to study.

Floating point

  • Normalized float \(\pm(1.b_1\ldots b_t)_2\times2^e\), hidden bit, \(t\) bits after the point.
  • \(k\) exponent bits: field 0 = zero and subnormals \((0.f)_2\times2^{e_{\min}}\); field \(2^k-1\) = Inf/NaN; bias \(=2^{k-1}-1\); \(e\in[1-\text{bias},\ 2^k-2-\text{bias}]\).
  • realmax \(=(2-2^{-t})\,2^{e_{\max}}\); realmin \(=2^{e_{\min}}\); smallest subnormal \(=2^{-t}\,2^{e_{\min}}\).
  • Gap in \([2^e,2^{e+1})\) is \(2^{e-t}\); gap after 1 is \(2^{-t}\); \(u=2^{-(t+1)}\).
  • \(\mathrm{fl}(x)=x(1+\delta)\), \(|\delta|\le u\), in the normalized range. \(\mathrm{fl}(x\text{ op }y)=(x\text{ op }y)(1+\delta)\) needs 2 guard bits and a sticky bit.
  • Round to nearest; on a tie, keep the neighbor whose last bit is 0.
  • Double: 1 | 11 | 52 bits, bias 1023, \(e\in[-1022,1023]\), realmax \(\approx1.8\times10^{308}\), realmin \(\approx2.2\times10^{-308}\), \(2^{-1074}\approx4.9\times10^{-324}\), \(u=2^{-53}\approx1.1\times10^{-16}\).
  • \(n\)-bit two's complement: \([-2^{n-1},\,2^{n-1}-1]\); negate by flipping bits and adding 1.
  • Conversions: integers divide by 2 (read remainders last to first); fractions multiply by 2 (read integer parts first to last); for \((0.\overline{b_1\ldots b_p})_2\), multiply by \(2^p\) and subtract.
  • Cancellation: the subtraction is exact but exposes earlier errors. Quadratic: \(b\gt0\Rightarrow x_2=\frac{-b-\sqrt D}{2a}\), \(x_1=\frac{c}{a x_2}\). Scale by \(\max(|a|,|b|,|c|)\) to avoid overflow.

Nonlinear equations

  • Bisection: needs continuity and a sign change. After \(k\) steps \(|c-r|\le\frac{b-a}{2^{k+1}}\). \(p\) decimals means error \(\lt5\times10^{-(p+1)}\). Linear, and independent of \(f\).
  • Taylor: \(f(b)=f(a)+(b-a)f'(a)+\frac{(b-a)^2}{2}f''(s)\).
  • Newton: \(x_{k+1}=x_k-\frac{f(x_k)}{f'(x_k)}\). Quadratic if \(f'(x_*)\ne0\) and \(x_0\) is close: \(e_{k+1}\approx\left|\frac{f''(x_*)}{2f'(x_*)}\right|e_k^2\). Multiplicity \(m\): linear, \(C=1-\frac1m\).
  • Newton's iteration function: \(g'(x)=\frac{f(x)f''(x)}{f'(x)^2}\), so \(g'(x_*)=0\).
  • Fixed point \(x_{k+1}=g(x_k)\): converges near \(x_*\) if \(|g'(x_*)|\lt1\); \(e_{k+1}\approx|g'(x_*)|\,e_k\) (linear); quadratic if \(g'(x_*)=0\). On \([a,b]\): \(e_k\le\rho^ke_0\), \(\rho=\max|g'|\).
  • Secant: \(x_{k+1}=x_k-\frac{f(x_k)(x_k-x_{k-1})}{f(x_k)-f(x_{k-1})}\); \(e_{k+1}\approx C\,e_ke_{k-1}\); order \(\frac{1+\sqrt5}2\approx1.618\).
  • Order from data: \(r\approx\frac{\log(e_{k+1}/e_k)}{\log(e_k/e_{k-1})}\).
  • Global: bisection. Local: Newton, secant. fzero = bisection + secant + inverse quadratic interpolation.
  • Calculator: \(\log_2N=\ln N/\ln2\); set radians for trig.
